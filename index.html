  1. The Ambitious Guest

    A compact story about ambition meeting contingency. I like it as a small reminder that plans can be elegant and still be at the mercy of terrain, timing, and luck.

    Nathaniel Hawthorne / 3/5
  2. Crime and Punishment

    I can respect the psychological intensity without pretending it was a favorite. The most useful part for me was how relentlessly the book studies rationalization, guilt, and the unstable stories people tell themselves.

    Fyodor Dostoevsky / 2/5
  3. A Time to Kill (Jake Brigance, #1)

    A courtroom novel with real momentum. What stayed with me is the pressure it puts on law as both procedure and moral theatre, where institutions are necessary but never emotionally neutral.

    John Grisham / 4/5
  4. Golden Son (Red Rising Saga, #2)

    The series becomes much larger here: strategy, class, betrayal, political violence, and the cost of moving from rebellion to power. It is sharp, fast, and structurally satisfying.

    Pierce Brown / 5/5
  5. Morning Star (Red Rising Saga, #3)

    A strong close to the first Red Rising arc because it keeps the emotional stakes tied to institutional change. The action works, but the better question is what kind of order can survive after revolt.

    Pierce Brown / 5/5
  6. Red Rising (Red Rising Saga, #1)

    A brutal entry point into a caste system built as a machine. The book is at its best when the spectacle reveals incentives, hierarchy, and the training of people to accept a designed world.

    Pierce Brown / 4/5
